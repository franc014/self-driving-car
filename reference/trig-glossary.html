Trigonometry glossary

The vocabulary used in this workspace, defined for this project rather than for a textbook. Terms are listed roughly from most basic to most applied.

How to use this

When a lesson uses a word you cannot define in one sentence, come back here. The “in this project” line is the one that matters for reading car.js.

Angle
The amount of rotation between two directions. In this project, car.angle is measured in radians from straight up; a positive angle turns the car left. See the sign decoder.
Radian
The angle whose arc length equals the circle's radius. A full turn is 2π radians. JavaScript's Math.sin and Math.cos take radians, and the car's steering increment is 0.03 rad. See “Radians in one breath.”
Degree
A unit of angle equal to 1/360 of a full turn. 90° = π/2 radians. Degrees are convenient for humans; radians are what the math library wants. Convert with degrees * Math.PI / 180.
Sine (sin)
The ratio of the side opposite an angle to the hypotenuse. In the car's velocity triangle, sine is the sideways share of the speed: left = speed · sin θ. See the values table.
Cosine (cos)
The ratio of the side adjacent to an angle to the hypotenuse. In the car's triangle, cosine is the up share of the speed: up = speed · cos θ. See the values table.
Hypotenuse
The longest side of a right triangle, opposite the right angle. Here it is the velocity vector: its length is the speed, and its direction is the heading. See the velocity triangle.
Opposite
The side across from the angle you are measuring. Relative to the car's angle θ, the opposite side is the horizontal leg — the sideways movement. Sine uses this side.
Adjacent
The side that touches the angle you are measuring, other than the hypotenuse. Relative to the car's angle θ, the adjacent side is the vertical leg — the up movement. Cosine uses this side.
Unit circle
A circle of radius 1 centered on the origin. Every direction corresponds to one point on it. In this project, speed 1 gives the point (−sin θ, −cos θ), because the circle starts at up and the screen's y axis points down. See the unit-circle section.
Vector
A quantity with both magnitude and direction. The car's velocity is a vector; its position change (Δx, Δy) is a vector. A bare number like speed is a scalar.
Component
The part of a vector along one axis. The car's movement has an x component Δx and a y component Δy. Sine and cosine are the tools that produce them. See the decomposition.
Magnitude
The length of a vector, ignoring its direction. The magnitude of the car's velocity is its speed. If you know Δx and Δy, the distance moved is Math.hypot(Δx, Δy).
Heading
The direction the car's nose points. In this project, heading is stored as this.angle; the movement vector is always along the heading. See how to compute a heading from a target.
Coordinate system
The axes and sign conventions used to describe positions. On a canvas, the origin is top-left, x increases right, and y increases down. This is why the movement code subtracts both components. See the sign conventions.
Counterclockwise
Opposite to the direction clock hands move. On screen, a positive car angle turns counterclockwise — left. The left arrow adds to the angle.
Clockwise
In the direction clock hands move. Canvas's positive rotation is clockwise because its y axis points down. The car is drawn with rotate(−θ) to convert its counterclockwise angle into a canvas rotation. See the draw pattern.
Periodicity
The property of sine and cosine that repeats every 2π. The car's angle can grow past a full turn or go negative; the direction simply repeats, so the formulas keep working without normalization.
atan2
An inverse tangent that takes the sine and cosine separately, so it can distinguish all four quadrants. Use Math.atan2(-dx, -dy) to turn a screen-space direction back into the car's angle. See the inverse formula.
Speed
How fast the car moves along its heading, in pixels per frame. In this code, speed is signed: positive is forward, negative is reverse. It is not the same as velocity, which also includes direction.
Velocity
Speed together with a direction. The car stores speed and angle separately, then combines them into a velocity vector with sine and cosine. See the velocity triangle.
Displacement
The change in position over one frame: (Δx, Δy). The car adds a displacement to its position every frame; a path is the sum of many small displacements.
Lane
One of the equal-width strips the road is divided into. A road with laneCount = 3 has three lanes, indexed 0, 1, and 2. See the lane formulas.
Lane width
road.width / road.laneCount. The width of one lane, not the thickness of a painted line. At width 360 and 3 lanes, the lane width is 120.
Lane center
The x coordinate halfway across a lane, where the car is placed by getLaneCenter(index). It is half a lane inside the lane's left boundary. See the placement table.
Lane divider
The painted line between two lanes. With 3 lanes there are 2 dividers. A divider is at a whole-lane fraction i / laneCount; a lane center is half a lane further in. See the half-lane distinction.
Linear interpolation (lerp)
Returning a point a given fraction of the way from one value to another: lerp(A, B, t) = A + (B − A) · t. At t = 0 the result is A; at t = 1 it is B. Values outside 0–1 extrapolate. See the lerp reference.
Parameter t
The fraction passed to lerp. It is not a pixel count. t = 0.5 means halfway across the interval; the same fraction works after the road width changes.
Segment
A straight piece of line defined by two endpoints. The road borders are stored as segments so a sensor ray can later intersect them. See the border structure.
Sensor
The car's artificial proximity detector. In this project it is a fan of rays; each ray finds the nearest segment it crosses within rayLength, and the result is stored as a reading. See the sensor fan.
Ray
A start point plus a direction and a maximum length. It is a segment whose second endpoint is computed as start + length · direction. See the ray endpoint.
Ray casting
Finding what a ray meets first by testing it against scene geometry. In this project the geometry is a list of segments, not a pixel grid. See the intersection test.
Ray fan
The set of rays produced by sweeping an angle from +raySpread/2 to −raySpread/2 with lerp, then adding car.angle. The fan is rigid: turning the car turns every ray. See the fan table.
Spread
The total angle between the first and last ray of a fan, not the gap between neighbours. This branch uses Math.PI / 2, which is 90°. The gap is spread / (rayCount − 1). See the 8-ray fan.
Unit vector
A vector of length 1. The car's direction (−sin θ, −cos θ) is a unit vector because sin²θ + cos²θ = 1. Multiplying it by a length produces a vector of that length. See the unit-vector proof.
Direction vector
The vector that says where a line or ray points, without its length. For a ray A → B, the direction is r = B − A. For a border C → D, it is s = D − C. See the three vectors.
Scalar multiplication
Multiplying a vector by a single number: k · (x, y) = (k·x, k·y). It changes length but not direction (a negative k also reverses direction). The ray endpoint uses rayLength · direction.
Vector subtraction
B − A = (B.x − A.x, B.y − A.y), the arrow from A to B. It turns two points into a direction vector. See the vector toolkit.
Cross product (2D)
p × q = p.x·q.y − p.y·q.x. It returns one number: the signed area of the parallelogram spanned by p and q. Zero means the vectors are parallel. getIntersection uses three of these as tTop, uTop, and bottom. Drag the area in the vector lab. See the derivation.
Determinant
Another name for the signed-area number produced by the 2D cross product. In the intersection formula the determinant is the denominator; dividing by it cancels the area and leaves a fraction. See why the denominator is an area.
Parametric equation
Writing a point as a start plus a fraction of a direction: P(t) = A + t(B − A). The same t applies to x and y, so the point stays on the straight segment. See the two segment equations.
Intersection
A point shared by two geometric objects. For two segments, it exists only when both fractions are inside [0, 1]. See the bounds check.
Offset
The t value returned by getIntersection. It is the fraction along the ray, so distance is offset × rayLength. Comparing offsets is how the sensor chooses the nearest hit. See the nearest-hit code.
Reading
One ray's result for one frame: the nearest touch object, or null when no segment is within range. The sensor stores one reading per ray in this.readings. See the reading loop.
Occlusion
Hiding something behind a nearer object. A sensor keeps only the smallest offset, so everything beyond the first hit is occluded. A traffic car in front of a border hides the border. See nearest-hit selection.
Polygon
A closed shape described by a list of points. Each pair of consecutive points is an edge; the last point connects back to the first. A future traffic car is a polygon. See the object table.
Edge
One side of a polygon, represented as a segment. The sensor tests every edge with the same getIntersection function it uses for road borders.
Collision detection
Testing whether two shapes overlap. For two car polygons, test every edge of one against every edge of the other. It is the same segment-intersection test with a different caller. See the reuse table.
World coordinates
The coordinates the road and car store, independent of the viewport. The camera translates world coordinates into screen coordinates at draw time. See the camera section.
Camera
The draw-time transform that decides which part of the world is visible. In this project it is a vertical translation that keeps the car at 70% of the viewport height, so the road appears to scroll past.
Practical infinity
A very large finite number (1000000) used to make the road longer than any viewport. Preferred over Infinity because infinite values can turn intersection arithmetic into NaN.
